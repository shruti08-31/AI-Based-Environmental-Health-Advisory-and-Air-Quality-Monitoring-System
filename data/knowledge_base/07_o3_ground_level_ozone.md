# Ground-Level Ozone ($O_3$) & Photochemical Formation
Source: WHO Global Air Quality Guidelines & US EPA Ground-Level Ozone Reference

Tropospheric (ground-level) ozone ($O_3$) is a secondary pollutant formed when nitrogen oxides ($NO_x$) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) react in the presence of sunlight. Consequently, $O_3$ levels frequently peak during sunny afternoon hours and warm stagnant weather. Ozone is a strong oxidant that causes airway irritation, coughing, chest tightness, and temporary decreases in lung capacity during outdoor exercise.

---

## 1. Stratospheric vs. Tropospheric Ozone ("Good Up High, Bad Nearby")
Although chemically identical ($O_3$, a triatomic allotrope of oxygen), ozone's environmental and health impact depends entirely on its altitude:
* **Stratospheric Ozone (15–50 km altitude):** Forms naturally via ultraviolet (UV-C) photolysis of molecular oxygen ($O_2$). It forms the protective **ozone layer**, absorbing harmful solar UV-B radiation before it reaches Earth's surface.
* **Tropospheric / Ground-Level Ozone (0–2 km boundary layer):** A harmful secondary air pollutant and potent greenhouse gas generated through sunlight-driven photochemical reactions between anthropogenic and biogenic precursor gases.

---

## 2. Photochemical Reaction Mechanism & Diurnal Cycle
Unlike primary pollutants ($SO_2$, $CO$, primary $PM$), ground-level ozone is **not emitted directly** from smokestacks or tailpipes. Its formation requires three ingredients: **nitrogen oxides ($NO_x = NO + NO_2$)**, **volatile organic compounds (VOCs)**, and **ultraviolet solar radiation ($h\nu$, $\lambda < 420\,\text{nm}$)**:

1. **Photolysis of $NO_2$:** Morning sunlight splits nitrogen dioxide into nitric oxide and a free oxygen atom:
   $$NO_2 + h\nu \rightarrow NO + O(^3P)$$
2. **Ozone Generation:** The reactive oxygen atom rapidly combines with molecular oxygen ($O_2$) in the presence of a stabilizing third body ($M$, such as $N_2$ or $O_2$):
   $$O(^3P) + O_2 + M \rightarrow O_3 + M$$
3. **The VOC Catalytic Loop (Net Accumulation):** In clean air without VOCs, newly formed $O_3$ simply reacts back with $NO$ to regenerate $NO_2$ ($NO + O_3 \rightarrow NO_2 + O_2$), establishing a steady-state **photostationary equilibrium** with no net ozone buildup. However, when **VOCs** (from vehicle fuel evaporation, industrial solvents, paints, and vegetation) oxidize via hydroxyl radicals ($OH^\bullet$), they produce peroxy radicals ($HO_2^\bullet$ and $RO_2^\bullet$). These radicals convert $NO$ back into $NO_2$ **without consuming $O_3$**, breaking the equilibrium and driving rapid daytime $O_3$ accumulation.

### Why Ozone Peaks in Sunny Afternoons & Downwind Suburbs
* **Afternoon Peak:** Precursor emissions ($NO_x$ and VOCs) build up during morning rush-hour traffic; as solar radiation intensity and ambient temperatures climb between **12:00 PM and 4:00 PM**, photochemical reaction rates accelerate, producing maximum 1-hour and 8-hour $O_3$ concentrations in mid-to-late afternoon.
* **Nighttime Titration & Rural/Suburban Accumulation:** After sunset, photolysis stops. Along busy urban roads, fresh tailpipe nitric oxide ($NO$) rapidly destroys local ozone ($NO + O_3 \rightarrow NO_2 + O_2$), a process called **$NO_x$ titration**. Conversely, in downwind suburban and rural areas—where fresh $NO$ emissions are scarce—accumulated ozone plumes persist longer into the evening.
* **VOC-Limited vs. $NO_x$-Limited Regimes:** In dense urban cores saturated with $NO_x$ (**VOC-limited** regime), reducing $NO_x$ alone without cutting VOCs can temporarily increase local $O_3$ by reducing titration (the "weekend ozone effect"). In suburban and regional airsheds (**$NO_x$-limited** regime), reducing $NO_x$ directly suppresses ozone production.

---

## 3. Precursor Emission Sources
* **Nitrogen Oxides ($NO_x$):** Motor vehicle exhaust (diesel and petrol), thermal power plants, industrial boilers, and off-road construction machinery.
* **Anthropogenic VOCs:** Gasoline evaporation and refueling vapors, petrochemical refining, industrial solvents, paints/coatings, chemical manufacturing, and open waste burning.
* **Biogenic VOCs (BVOCs):** Isoprene and monoterpenes emitted naturally by trees and vegetation during hot, sunny weather—which explain why heatwaves frequently trigger regional ozone episodes when biogenic VOCs mix with urban $NO_x$ plumes.

## 4. Regulatory Standards & CPCB Breakpoints for Ground-Level $O_3$
Because acute respiratory effects depend on cumulative multi-hour exposure during daytime activity, ambient standards and AQI scales evaluate $O_3$ over an **8-hour rolling average** (with a 1-hour threshold for extreme episodic spikes):

| Regulatory / Guideline Framework | Averaging Period | Concentration Limit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **WHO 2021 Air Quality Guideline (AQG)** | 8-hour daily maximum | $100\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ ($\approx 50\,\text{ppb}$) |
| **WHO 2021 Peak Season Guideline** | Peak-season mean of daily max 8-hr | $60\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ ($\approx 30\,\text{ppb}$) |
| **India CPCB NAAQS (All Area Categories)** | 8-hour | $100\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ |
| **India CPCB NAAQS (All Area Categories)** | 1-hour | $180\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ |
| **US EPA Primary & Secondary NAAQS** | 8-hour (4th highest daily max) | $70\,\text{ppb}$ ($\approx 137\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$) |

### CPCB IND-AQI Breakpoints for $O_3$
* **Good (0–50):** $0\text{–}50\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ (8-hour)
* **Satisfactory (51–100):** $51\text{–}100\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ (8-hour)
* **Moderate (101–200):** $101\text{–}168\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ (8-hour)
* **Poor (201–300):** $169\text{–}208\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ (8-hour)
* **Very Poor (301–400):** $209\text{–}748\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ (evaluated on 1-hour average)
* **Severe (401–500):** $> 748\,\mu\text{g/m}^3$ (evaluated on 1-hour average)

## 5. Toxicological Mechanisms & Clinical Health Effects
Ozone is poorly soluble in water, allowing it to bypass upper nasal scrubbing and penetrate deeply into the **conducting airways, terminal bronchioles, and proximal alveoli**:
1. **Lipid Peroxidation & Epithelial Injury:** As a powerful oxidant, $O_3$ reacts directly with unsaturated fatty acids and antioxidants in the respiratory tract lining fluid, generating toxic ozonides and lipid hydroperoxides that injure airway epithelial cells.
2. **Neural Reflexes & Transient Lung Function Decline:** Stimulation of pulmonary C-fiber sensory receptors triggers involuntary shallow breathing, coughing, substernal pain upon deep inspiration, and measurable reductions in Forced Expiratory Volume ($FEV_1$) and Forced Vital Capacity ($FVC$) within 1 to 2 hours of exposure.
3. **Neutrophilic Airway Inflammation & Hyperresponsiveness:** Within 6 to 24 hours post-exposure, neutrophils infiltrate the airways, increasing bronchial sensitivity to aeroallergens, cold air, and sulfur dioxide ($SO_2$), and exacerbating asthma and COPD.
4. **Long-Term & Ecosystem Impacts:** Repeated peak-season ozone exposure contributes to permanent small-airway remodeling, chronic respiratory mortality, and severe **agricultural crop yield losses** (damaging stomata and photosynthesis in wheat, rice, and soybeans).

## 6. Vulnerable Groups & Exposure Reduction Strategies
* **High-Risk Populations:**
  * **People with Asthma & Chronic Lung Disease (COPD):** Experience heightened airway inflammation, wheezing, and increased emergency department visits during afternoon ozone spikes.
  * **Children & Adolescents:** Spend more time outdoors during afternoon hours, breathe faster per unit body weight, and have developing lungs.
  * **Active Outdoor Exercisers & Outdoor Workers:** Heavy physical exertion increases minute ventilation 5- to 10-fold and shifts breathing from nose to mouth, dramatically increasing internal ozone dose—even in otherwise healthy athletes.
* **Evidence-Based Protective Measures:**
  * **Schedule Outdoor Exercise in the Morning or After Sunset:** Because $O_3$ is sunlight-dependent, ambient concentrations are lowest in the **early morning** and late evening; shifting running, cycling, or sports practice away from 12:00 PM–5:00 PM cuts ozone dose substantially.
  * **Indoor Shelter Works Effectively for Ozone:** Unlike $PM_{2.5}$, ozone is highly reactive and decays rapidly upon contact with indoor walls, carpets, and furniture, making indoor $O_3$ concentrations typically **50% to 80% lower** than outdoor levels when windows are closed.
  * **Respirator Limitation:** Standard **N95/FFP2 particulate respirators do not filter ozone gas**; effective filtration requires activated-carbon media, though staying indoors during afternoon peaks is far more effective.
