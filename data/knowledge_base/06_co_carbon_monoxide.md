# Carbon Monoxide ($CO$) & Incomplete Combustion
Source: WHO Global Air Quality Guidelines & US EPA Carbon Monoxide Reference

Carbon monoxide ($CO$) is an odorless, colorless, tasteless, and non-irritating gas generated by the incomplete combustion of carbon-based fuels in petrol/diesel vehicles, generators, stoves, and biomass burning. When inhaled, $CO$ diffuses across the pulmonary alveoli and binds to hemoglobin with much higher affinity than oxygen to form **carboxyhemoglobin ($COHb$)**, reducing oxygen delivery to vital organs such as the heart and brain. Individuals with coronary artery disease are particularly vulnerable to chest pain (angina) during elevated $CO$ exposure.

---

## 1. Chemical Formation & Atmospheric Behavior
Carbon monoxide forms whenever carbon-containing fuels (gasoline, diesel, coal, natural gas, wood, crop residue, charcoal) burn in an oxygen-deficient environment or at temperatures too low for complete oxidation to carbon dioxide ($CO_2$):
* **Incomplete Combustion Chemistry:**
  $$2C + O_2 \rightarrow 2CO \quad (\text{fuel-rich / oxygen-limited combustion})$$
  Cold engine starts, poorly tuned carburetors/fuel injectors, idling traffic in congested canyons, and smoldering biomass fires produce disproportionately high $CO$ emissions.
* **Atmospheric Residence Time & Sinks:** Unlike highly reactive gases ($O_3$, $NO_2$), $CO$ is chemically stable in the lower troposphere with an atmospheric lifetime of **1 to 3 months**. Its primary atmospheric sink is slow oxidation by hydroxyl radicals ($OH^\bullet$) to form carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), a reaction chain that also indirectly contributes to background tropospheric ozone production.
* **Spatial & Diurnal Patterns:** Because ambient $CO$ is predominantly driven by motor vehicle exhaust, concentrations peak sharply along **congested roadways, intersections, tunnels, and underground parking garages**, following a bimodal diurnal curve aligned with morning and evening rush hours.

---

## 2. Primary Emission Sources (Outdoor vs. Indoor)
* **Ambient (Outdoor) Sources:**
  * **On-Road Motor Vehicles:** Petrol (gasoline) engines—especially during cold starts, acceleration, and stop-and-go congestion—account for the vast majority of urban ambient $CO$.
  * **Non-Road Engines & Industrial Combustion:** Diesel/petrol backup generators, construction equipment, metal smelting, refineries, and brick kilns.
  * **Biomass & Wildfires:** Agricultural crop-residue burning, forest fires, and open municipal waste burning.
* **Indoor & Micro-Environmental Sources (Highest Acute Risk):**
  * Unvented or malfunctioning gas stoves, space heaters, water heaters, and kerosene lamps.
  * Indoor or semi-enclosed operation of portable backup generators, charcoal grills, or solid-fuel cookstoves (wood, dung, coal) without chimney ventilation.
  * Vehicle exhaust seeping from attached garages into living spaces.

## 4. Toxicological Mechanisms & Physiological Effects
Because $CO$ is non-irritating to the eyes or respiratory tract, exposed individuals receive no sensory warning before systemic hypoxia develops:
1. **Competitive Hemoglobin Binding ($COHb$ Formation):** $CO$ crosses the alveolar-capillary membrane and binds to iron sites on hemoglobin with an affinity **200 to 250 times greater than oxygen ($O_2$)**, forming **carboxyhemoglobin ($COHb$)** and directly reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity.
2. **Leftward Shift of the Oxyhemoglobin Dissociation Curve:** Binding of $CO$ to one of hemoglobin's four heme subunits alters the protein conformation, increasing the affinity of the remaining subunits for oxygen. This prevents hemoglobin from releasing oxygen to peripheral tissues even when arterial oxygen tension appears normal.
3. **Cellular Respiration Inhibition:** $CO$ also binds to intracellular heme proteins, including **myoglobin** in cardiac/skeletal muscle and **cytochrome c oxidase** in mitochondria, impairing cellular ATP production and triggering oxidative stress.

### Dose-Response by Carboxyhemoglobin ($COHb$) Saturation
* **$< 1\%\text{–}2\%$ (Normal Endogenous Baseline):** Produced naturally by heme catabolism; smokers typically maintain $3\%\text{–}8\%$ $COHb$.
* **$2\%\text{–}5\%$ (Low Ambient / Traffic Exposure):** Reduced exercise tolerance and earlier onset of **myocardial ischemia and chest pain (angina)** in patients with coronary artery disease; subtle impairments in visual perception and vigilance.
* **$10\%\text{–}20\%$ (Moderate Exposure):** Throbbing headache, dizziness, fatigue, shortness of breath on exertion, and nausea.
* **$20\%\text{–}40\%$ (Severe Poisoning):** Confusion, visual disturbances, syncope (fainting), tachycardia, and cardiac arrhythmias.
* **$> 50\%$ (Life-Threatening):** Seizures, coma, respiratory failure, and death.

---

## 5. Vulnerable Populations & Exposure Reduction
* **High-Risk Groups:**
  * **Individuals with Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) or Heart Failure:** The myocardium extracts a high fraction of oxygen at rest and cannot easily compensate for reduced oxygen delivery, making angina and arrhythmias the earliest clinical manifestations of ambient $CO$.
  * **Fetuses & Pregnant Individuals:** Fetal hemoglobin ($HbF$) binds $CO$ even more tightly than adult hemoglobin ($HbA$) and clears $CO$ more slowly, increasing risks of fetal hypoxia and low birth weight.
  * **Individuals with Anemia or Chronic Lung Disease (COPD):** Already reduced baseline oxygen-carrying capacity or gas exchange magnifies tissue hypoxia.
  * **Traffic Police, Toll-Booth Operators & Roadside Workers:** Prolonged occupational exposure near idling engines.
* **Mitigation & Safety Controls:**
  * **Mask & Filter Ineffectiveness:** **N95, surgical, and standard activated-carbon masks provide zero protection against $CO$**, because $CO$ is a small, non-polar gas molecule that passes freely through particulate and standard carbon filters.
  * **Source & Behavioral Controls:** Avoid vigorous exercise immediately alongside congested roadways during rush hours; never run fuel-burning generators, vehicles, or charcoal stoves indoors or near open windows; maintain catalytic converters and engine tuning; install UL-listed **electrochemical Carbon Monoxide alarms** in indoor living areas with fuel-burning appliances.
