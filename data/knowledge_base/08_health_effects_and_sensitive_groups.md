# Health Effects & Vulnerable / Sensitive Population Groups
Source: WHO & Public-Health Respiratory Protection Guidance

Air pollution affects both healthy individuals during intense exposure and vulnerable groups at lower thresholds. Sensitive populations include people with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular disease, children (whose lungs are still developing and who breathe more air per unit body weight), older adults, and pregnant individuals. Public-health advisories provide preventive risk reduction guidance and never replace clinical medical diagnosis.

---

## 1. Physiological Determinants of Susceptibility
Individual vulnerability to ambient air pollution depends on biological, developmental, and behavioral factors that alter either the **internal inhaled dose** or the body's **physiological capacity to neutralize oxidative stress**:
* **Ventilation Rate & Breathing Route:** Physical exertion increases minute ventilation from ~6 L/min at rest to 40–100 L/min during vigorous aerobic exercise and shifts breathing from the nose to the mouth, bypassing nasal filtration and increasing deep alveolar deposition by 5- to 10-fold.
* **Developmental Windows:** Incomplete alveolarization and immature antioxidant defense systems in infants and children amplify tissue injury from fine particulates ($PM_{2.5}$) and reactive gases ($NO_2$, $O_3$).
* **Pre-Existing Inflammatory & Vascular Burden:** Chronic airway inflammation (asthma, COPD) or endothelial dysfunction (coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes) lowers the threshold at which acute pollution spikes trigger clinical decompensation.

---

## 2. Population-Specific Risks & Clinical Vulnerabilities

| Population Group | Primary Physiological Vulnerability | Associated Short- & Long-Term Clinical Outcomes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **People with Asthma** | Heightened bronchial hyperreactivity, airway smooth-muscle sensitivity, and impaired mucociliary clearance | Acute bronchoconstriction, coughing, wheezing, chest tightness, increased rescue bronchodilator use, and emergency department visits (triggered strongly by $PM_{2.5}$, $SO_2$, $NO_2$, and $O_3$) |
| **People with COPD / Chronic Bronchitis** | Reduced pulmonary reserve, chronic airway narrowing, and impaired alveolar macrophage clearance | Acute infective and non-infective COPD exacerbations, hypoxemia, hospitalization, and accelerated decline in lung function ($FEV_1$) |
| **People with Cardiovascular Disease** | Endothelial dysfunction, systemic oxidative stress, increased blood viscosity, and autonomic nervous system imbalance | Acute myocardial ischemia (angina triggered by $CO$ and $PM_{2.5}$), heart attacks (myocardial infarction), ischemic stroke, cardiac arrhythmias, and heart failure decompensation |
| **Children & Infants** | Higher minute ventilation per kilogram of body weight, ongoing lung and alveolar development, and greater daytime outdoor activity | Higher incidence of new-onset childhood asthma, stunted lung growth, acute lower respiratory tract infections (pneumonia, bronchiolitis), and school absenteeism |
| **Older Adults (65+ Years)** | Age-related decline in lung elasticity and antioxidant capacity, plus high prevalence of subclinical heart and lung disease | Elevated short-term cardiorespiratory hospital admissions, stroke, arrhythmias, and accelerated cognitive decline |
| **Pregnant Individuals & Developing Fetuses** | Increased maternal minute ventilation (~20%–40% rise during pregnancy) and placental susceptibility to oxidative stress and $CO$ binding to fetal hemoglobin | Increased risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age infants, and gestational hypertension/preeclampsia |
| **Outdoor Workers & Athletes** | Prolonged cumulative exposure duration combined with elevated respiratory minute volume during exertion | Acute airway irritation, transient drops in $FEV_1$ and $FVC$, reduced aerobic performance, and higher cumulative cardiopulmonary disease burden |

## 3. Tiered Public-Health Activity Guidance by CPCB AQI Category
Public-health advisories translate ambient AQI levels into actionable behavioral adjustments, differentiating between **sensitive groups** and the **general healthy population**:

| CPCB AQI Category (Range) | Guidance for Sensitive & Vulnerable Groups | Guidance for General Healthy Population |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Good (0–50)** | Normal outdoor activities are safe; follow routine medication plans. | Normal outdoor activities and vigorous exercise are safe. |
| **Satisfactory (51–100)** | Unusually sensitive individuals (e.g., severe asthma) should monitor for minor breathing discomfort during prolonged outdoor exertion. | Normal outdoor activities are safe. |
| **Moderate (101–200)** | Reduce prolonged or heavy outdoor exertion; take more frequent breaks and keep rescue inhalers accessible. Schedule outdoor walks away from peak traffic and afternoon $O_3$ peaks. | Generally safe for routine activities; consider shortening prolonged high-intensity outdoor workouts if throat irritation or coughing occurs. |
| **Poor (201–300)** | Avoid prolonged or heavy outdoor exertion; move exercise indoors; keep windows closed during pollution spikes. | Reduce prolonged or heavy outdoor exertion (e.g., long-distance running or cycling); substitute lighter or indoor activities. |
| **Very Poor (301–400)** | Avoid all outdoor physical exertion; remain indoors with clean air filtration; wear a well-fitted **N95/FFP2** respirator if outdoor travel is unavoidable. | Avoid prolonged or heavy outdoor exertion; limit outdoor time; wear a well-fitted **N95/FFP2** respirator during extended outdoor exposure. |
| **Severe (401–500)** | Remain strictly indoors; run HEPA air purifiers if available; seek prompt medical evaluation if respiratory or cardiac symptoms worsen. | Avoid all outdoor physical exertion; remain indoors as much as possible; use a well-fitted **N95/FFP2** respirator if stepping outside is necessary. |

## 4. Evidence-Based Personal & Indoor Protective Controls
* **Respirator Selection & Limitations:**
  * **Effective for Particulates ($PM_{2.5}$, $PM_{10}$):** Properly sealed **N95, KN95, or FFP2** particulate respirators filter $\ge 95\%$ of airborne particles down to $0.3\,\mu\text{m}$. Facial hair or loose straps compromise the perimeter seal and markedly reduce protection.
  * **Ineffective for Gases:** Standard particulate respirators and cloth/surgical masks **do not filter gaseous pollutants** ($O_3$, $NO_2$, $SO_2$, $CO$). Avoiding high-traffic corridors and staying indoors during gas spikes is essential.
* **Indoor Air Quality Management:**
  * Close exterior windows and doors during outdoor pollution peaks, particularly during morning/evening thermal inversions or dust storms.
  * Operate portable air cleaners fitted with certified **True HEPA (H13/H14)** filters sized appropriately for the room's Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR); avoid ozone-generating "air purifiers" or ionizers.
  * Eliminate indoor pollutant generation by avoiding incense, scented candles, mosquito coils, tobacco smoke, and unvented solid-fuel or gas combustion.
  * Low-intensity indoor activities—such as **gentle yoga, stretching, or mobility exercises**—are safe alternatives to outdoor aerobic workouts when indoor air is clean and symptoms are absent.

---

## 5. Medical Disclaimer & Red-Flag Clinical Symptoms
Public-health air quality advisories provide **preventive environmental risk reduction** and **do not constitute medical diagnosis, clinical triage, or prescription guidance**. Individuals with chronic respiratory or cardiovascular conditions should follow personalized action plans established with their physician and never self-adjust prescription dosages or take unprescribed medications (such as antibiotics or leftover inhalers) based on air quality alerts.

**Seek urgent medical care immediately** if exposure to poor air quality is accompanied by any clinical red-flag symptoms:
* Severe shortness of breath, difficulty speaking in full sentences, or rapid breathing at rest.
* Persistent wheezing or asthma symptoms that fail to improve after using a prescribed rescue bronchodilator inhaler.
* Chest pain, pressure, tightness, palpitations, dizziness, fainting, or bluish discoloration of the lips or fingernails (cyanosis).
